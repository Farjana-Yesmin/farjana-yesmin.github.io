The Differential Diagnosis Gap: What a 21-Model Study Confirms About Arguable Systems

By Farjana Yesmin • 7 min read

A new study out of Mass General Brigham, published in JAMA Network Open, tested 21 large language models on 29 standardized clinical cases. The models included the usual names people expect to hear by now: GPT-5, Claude 4.5 Opus, Gemini 3.0, Grok 4. The result is not the one most vendor pages would like you to read.

Every single model got the final diagnosis right more than 90 percent of the time when it was handed all the relevant patient information up front. That is the number that ends up in a press release. But when the researchers looked earlier in the process, at the stage where a clinician builds a differential diagnosis from incomplete information, every model failed more than 80 percent of the time.

I have been writing about this exact split for almost a year now, usually from the angle of arguable systems and evidence synthesis. This study puts hard numbers next to something I had mostly been arguing on philosophical grounds. It is worth sitting with.

Why the Gap Exists

A final diagnosis is a lookup problem once you already have the labs, the imaging, and the history in front of you. Pattern match the case to the closest thing in the training distribution and you will usually land close to correct. A differential diagnosis is a different task entirely. The clinician has to generate a ranked list of plausible explanations before all the evidence is in, decide which tests would actually distinguish between them, and revise that list as new information arrives.

The researchers built a new scoring tool for this, called PrIME-LLM, that grades models across the separate stages of a clinical workflow instead of just checking whether the last line matches an answer key. That distinction matters more than it sounds. Most public benchmarks reward the model for arriving somewhere. Medicine cares just as much about how it got there.

The core finding, in plain terms:

Final diagnosis accuracy: over 90 percent across models.
Differential diagnosis failure rate: over 80 percent across the same models.
Same case. Same model. Two very different scores depending on which part of the reasoning chain you check.

This Is Not a New Model Problem, It Is a Visibility Problem

The corresponding author, Marc Succi, put it directly: these models are not ready for unsupervised clinical-grade deployment, and they need a human in the loop with close oversight. I agree with that conclusion, but I want to push on the word "oversight" for a moment, because it is doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence.

Oversight only functions if the thing being overseen is visible. If a model gives you a correct final answer and nothing else, there is nothing for a clinician to actually supervise. The reasoning that got skipped over, the alternative diagnoses that were never seriously weighed, none of that shows up in a single confident output. A clinician looking at a right answer with no visible reasoning trail cannot tell the difference between a model that reasoned well and got lucky, and one that reasoned badly and still landed on the right answer by proximity to its training data.

This is precisely the gap that arguable systems are meant to close. Not by making the model more accurate on the final answer, accuracy on that narrow metric is already high, but by forcing the intermediate reasoning into a form a clinician can actually contest. A visible, ranked differential diagnosis is not a nice-to-have explanation bolted on afterward. It is the actual clinical work. Skipping it and going straight to a final answer is the equivalent of a radiology report with no impression section, just a diagnosis with no path shown for how the reader arrived there.

Why This Matters More Outside Well-Resourced Hospitals

Mass General Brigham has the infrastructure to build a human-in-the-loop workflow with real oversight capacity. Most of the clinics I have worked with in Bangladesh do not have that luxury. A rural health worker using a triage tool cannot always call in a second opinion, and often does not have the time or training to interrogate a confident-sounding output even if the tool offered one. In that setting, a model that scores 90 percent on final diagnosis accuracy sounds like a success story right up until the 10 percent it misses lands on a case where the differential reasoning was never sound to begin with, and there was no one positioned to catch it. The failure rate on differential diagnosis is not a rounding error to fix later. It is the exact place where low-resource deployment is most exposed.

What I Take From This

Three things, mainly.

  • Benchmarks that only check the final answer will keep hiding this problem. Any evaluation framework worth trusting in medicine needs to score the intermediate reasoning stages separately, the way PrIME-LLM does.
  • "Human in the loop" is not a solution by itself. It only works when the system actually exposes something for the human to review, question, and overrule.
  • Low-resource settings need this more, not less. The places with the least capacity to catch a reasoning failure are the places most likely to be handed a tool that hides its reasoning entirely.

None of this is an argument against using these models in clinical settings. It is an argument for building the kind of scrutiny into the system before deployment, rather than hoping a busy clinician supplies it after the fact.
